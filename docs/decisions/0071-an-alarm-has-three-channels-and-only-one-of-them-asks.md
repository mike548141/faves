# 0071 — An alarm has three channels, and only one of them asks

**Status:** Accepted.

**Date:** 2026-08-17

## Context

Cook mode has had a one-tap countdown since 2026-08-16 ([ADR 0066] set what may
drive one). It ends in silence. A countdown that ends in silence is a countdown
you have to watch, and watching a phone for thirty-five minutes is the one thing
a cook cannot do — the recipe is on the bench precisely because your hands and
your eyes are somewhere else. So the timer was doing the arithmetic and leaving
the reader to do the job.

The owner ruled the whole shape on 2026-08-16 (ROADMAP 36d), going further than
the recommendation put to him. Three things arrive at once that this codebase
has never had: **audio**, **vibration**, and **a permission prompt**.

Each carries a different cost, and they are not comparable:

- **A tone costs bytes or it costs nothing.** An audio file is a precache entry,
  a line on the transfer budget and an asset to licence. A tone generated by an
  `OscillatorNode` is none of those, needs no network, and therefore works in
  flight mode on the first visit — which an mp3 fetched on demand would not.
- **A vibration costs nothing and, on the owner's own phone, does nothing.**
  iOS Safari has no vibration API at all. He was told and chose it anyway.
- **A permission costs something you can only spend once.** A notification
  prompt can be refused, and a refusal is sticky: it survives reloads and is
  undone only in per-site browser settings most people never find. [ADR 0069]
  says this at length about geolocation and it is the whole reason that record
  exists.

## Decision

**1. Every timer sounds and buzzes. Neither asks for anything.**

The tone is three 880 Hz beeps under a second, built in code
(`site/js/alarm.js`). Vibration is `navigator.vibrate`, feature-detected, and
its absence is never an error and never a warning.

**2. The AudioContext is armed by the tap that STARTS the timer.**

Autoplay policy suspends a context created outside a user gesture, and a
suspended context is silent at exactly the moment the bell is due — thirty-five
minutes after the last tap, with nothing on screen to say so. `arm()` therefore
runs synchronously inside the toggle's click handler, before anything is
awaited, because an `await` spends the gesture. That tap is already the reader
asking for a countdown, so nothing extra is asked of them.

**3. Only a timer of MORE than fifteen minutes gets a notification — and only
such a timer ever raises the permission prompt.**

Strictly greater, and the boundary is real rather than theoretical: two recipes
in the corpus state a step of exactly 900 seconds. The prompt is raised on the
tap that starts such a timer and at no other moment in the app's life. Nothing
is asked on page load, on opening cook mode, or on a short timer, whatever the
answer would have been.

The notification goes out through `registration.showNotification`, because
Chrome on Android refuses `new Notification()` outright; the page constructor is
the fallback, not the primary. `sw.js` gains a `notificationclick` handler that
focuses an already-open window on that recipe, navigates any other open window
of ours to it, and only opens a new one when nothing is open at all.

**4. The four permission states, in full.**

`Notification.permission` is a synchronous string, so — unlike geolocation,
which forced [ADR 0069] to reach for `navigator.permissions.query` — reading the
state costs nothing and prompts nobody.

| State | What happens |
| --- | --- |
| `granted` | The notification is raised. Nothing is asked, ever again. |
| `default` | The prompt is raised **at the moment a long timer starts**, once. |
| `denied` | No notification. A short line appears under a long timer's card saying notifications are blocked for this site, that the timer still sounds and vibrates, and that the browser's site settings can turn them back on. Stated once, in place, never a nag, never a modal. |
| API absent | Two channels, and **nothing said** — there is no setting behind it for the reader to go and change. |

The `denied` line is [ADR 0069]'s ruling applied here: *"silence about a choice
the visitor made is courtesy; silence about a state they may not know they are
in is a defect."*

**5. The bell reaches a reader who cannot hear it.**

The countdown numeral is `role="timer"`, whose implicit `aria-live: off` is
deliberate — a live countdown re-announces itself once a second and makes the
dialog unusable. So "time's up" reached no screen reader at all. A dedicated
`role="alert"` region, written only when a bell actually goes, is the third
channel. It says the translated "Time's up", the step, and the recipe.

**6. The bell rings for EVERY timer, not just the one on screen.**

Reading ahead while a bake runs is normal. The 1s interval now checks every
timer the session has created, and rings each at most once; a reset re-arms it.

## Rejected

- **A priming control — "tell me when this is done" — before the prompt.** The
  shape [ADR 0069] chose for location, and the one this record most owes an
  argument to. Rejected, and the reasons are specific rather than a preference
  for less work. (a) The owner ruled the trigger explicitly: *"ask at the moment
  a long timer starts"*. (b) The cost asymmetry that drove [ADR 0069] is much
  smaller here — a denied geolocation permission cost that feature **all** of
  its value, whereas a denied notification costs the third of three channels
  and the alarm still sounds and still buzzes. (c) The tap that starts a
  thirty-five-minute countdown *is* the priming action: it is user-initiated, it
  arrives deep in a session rather than on a cold first visit, and it is the
  reader stating the exact intention the permission serves. (d) A second control
  in a dialog worked with wet hands is a real cost, and this repo could not add
  one anyway without a stylesheet it did not own. 🚩 **The honest residue:** the
  browser's own prompt text says "this site wants to show notifications", not
  "so we can tell you the scones are done". A line of priming text would have
  said the second thing. If refusals turn out to be common, that line — not
  moving the prompt — is the fix, and it is the same conclusion [ADR 0069]
  reached from the other direction.
- **A shipped audio file.** A precache entry, a transfer-budget line and a
  licence question, in exchange for a nicer chime. `OscillatorNode` needs none
  of them and works offline on the first visit.
- **Notifying on every timer.** It would spend the prompt on a five-minute
  simmer you are standing over, and a refusal there costs the thirty-five-minute
  bake its notification forever. The scarce thing is the prompt, not the
  notification.
- **Suppressing the notification while the page is visible.** Not ruled on. The
  owner's ruling conditions the notification on duration and on nothing else,
  and inventing a second condition is inventing a rule. The consequence is
  named below and is his to settle.
- **Gating vibration on `prefers-reduced-motion`.** Considered because the brief
  asked for a quiet preference if one existed. `settings.js` has no sound,
  haptics or quiet key and Settings was ruled on 2026-08-16 to stay a drill-in
  rather than grow, so there was no precedent to follow. Reaching for
  `prefers-reduced-motion` instead was rejected on its merits: that preference
  is most often set for vestibular reasons, and vibration here is not decoration
  but the alarm channel a deaf reader depends on. Silencing it could leave
  someone with no perceivable bell at all.
- **A "your browser blocked notifications" toast.** `toast()` appends to
  `document.body`, which is inert and behind the backdrop while a modal
  `<dialog>` is open — it would have been invisible exactly where it was needed.

## Consequences

- **This is a permission prompt in an app that had none, and it is not the only
  one being built.** [ADR 0069]'s location ask was accepted the same week, in a
  different worktree, and neither had merged when this was written. Which lands
  first is a merge-order accident; neither record should claim to be the first,
  and this one does not. What matters is the shared constraint both inherit:
  a permission is requested for one purpose, used for that purpose, and is a
  licence for nothing else.
- **A notification will also appear when you are looking at the page.** Nothing
  suppresses it, per the rejection above. 🎯 **This is the owner's to settle** —
  a bell that fires on screen and in the notification shade at once is either
  reassuring or noise, and only he can say which.
- **A backgrounded tab's interval is throttled to roughly once a minute, and a
  suspended one stops.** The countdown stores the wall clock it ends at, so it
  is never *wrong*, but the bell can be up to about a minute late on a phone
  with the screen off — and on a fully frozen tab it waits until the tab wakes.
  Firing a notification on a schedule the OS holds needs the Notification
  Triggers API, which is not shipping anywhere. Returning to the recipe also
  re-checks, which is the half that is ours to fix.
- **iOS Safari gets two channels of three at best**, and one of those two — the
  vibration — does nothing. Known, accepted, recorded here so it is never later
  read as an oversight.
- **`site/js/alarm.js` joins the precache list in `sw.js`.** A new module on the
  shell's import graph, so `SHELL_VERSION` must move with it; the constant is
  the integrating session's to bump.
- **The reo dictionary does not yet carry `cook.notifyBlocked`.** `reo.js` falls
  back to the captured English, so the line is correct in English and untranslated
  in te reo Māori until the string is added.

## What a green check cannot show

`tools/cook_check.mjs` gained fifteen assertions driven through a real browser,
with `navigator.vibrate`, `AudioContext`, `Notification` and
`ServiceWorkerRegistration.showNotification` all **wrapped rather than
replaced**, so every count is a call the platform received. The notification
permission is set through CDP, so `granted` and `denied` are the browser's own
states. The only thing faked anywhere is the clock — a thirty-minute bake cannot
be waited out, and because the countdown stores the wall clock it ends at,
winding `Date.now` forward is exactly what a phone that slept through the bake
sees on waking.

None of that shows:

1. **That a sound came out.** There is no speaker. What is counted is the node
   graph the page built and scheduled.
2. **That a phone moved.** Desktop Chrome has `navigator.vibrate` and returns
   `false` — no motor. The call is real; the buzz is not. On the owner's own
   iPhone there will be no call at all.
3. **That a notification was drawn, survived being backgrounded, or that tapping
   it reopened the recipe.** Headless Chrome accepts the call and there is
   nothing to look at. The `notificationclick` handler in `sw.js` is exercised by
   nothing here; it needs a real phone.
4. **That any bell rings at all on a phone whose screen is off and whose tab the
   OS has frozen.** That is the case the notification exists FOR, and it is the
   one no browser on a laptop can reproduce.
5. **Whether three beeps at 880 Hz are audible over an extractor fan**, or
   pleasant, or too quiet. That is a kitchen question.

One assertion here was itself proved decorative before it shipped. "A bell rings
exactly once" passed with the ring-once guard **deleted**, because the tick that
rings the last running timer is also the tick that stops the interval — the
guard could not bite in a single-timer scenario. It was replaced by one that
starts a second, longer timer first, so the clock keeps ticking after the bell:
that version fails as it should. 🔑 The recurring lesson, now eleven times over:
a check that cannot fail is a check nobody reads, and the only way to find out
which kind you wrote is to break the thing it guards and watch.
